The premise is the whole appeal here. Divergent builds a society that sorts every person into exactly one quality, brave or smart or selfless or honest, and then organizes all of life around that one label. Tris doesn't come back clean on the test. She's more than one thing, which the system has no category for, and the entire plot follows from her refusing to pretend otherwise.

I find that setup more interesting than the usual dystopian setup, because the villain isn't really a person. It's the sorting. A system that needs everyone to be one thing will eventually treat the people who don't fit as a threat rather than an inconvenience, and the film is clear-eyed that this isn't malice so much as structure doing what structure does. Jeanine isn't scary because she's cruel. She's scary because she genuinely believes the categories are load-bearing and that anyone outside them has to go.

Tris is the reason it works. She's not a chosen one in the usual sense, she's just somebody who won't perform a version of herself that would be easier for everyone else. She takes the harder initiation, she takes the hits, and she keeps not fitting. Watching someone get punished for being more than their label and decide to keep being it anyway is a good story regardless of what genre you dress it in.

Four is the other half of that. Their relationship is built on the same idea, since he's hiding his own test result and his own history, and the two of them are basically the only people in the story not performing a faction. The scenes where they stop pretending with each other are better written than they needed to be for a film in this genre, and it's most of why the series holds up on a rewatch when the world-building starts showing its seams.
